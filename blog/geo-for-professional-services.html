GEO for professional services firms.

Industry24 March 20266 min readBy Isolation Media

Professional services firms have a harder GEO problem than product businesses, and it is not the one they usually think it is. The difficulty is not competition. It is that what they sell, judgement applied to a specific situation, resists being written down in a form an engine can quote.

The generality trap

Ask most firms what they do and you get something like "we provide tailored solutions to help our clients achieve their objectives". Every word of that is defensible and none of it is usable. An engine cannot recommend a firm for anything specific on the strength of it.

The firms winning answers have done something uncomfortable: they have stated plainly what they are for and, by implication, what they are not for. A firm known for one sector at one scale gets recommended precisely. A firm that could serve anyone gets recommended to no one.

Publish the thinking, not the credentials

Professional sites are heavy on qualifications and light on point of view. But credentials are table stakes and every competitor has them. What differentiates you, and what an engine can quote, is your actual position on the questions clients face.

  • What you think most firms get wrong about a common problem
  • How you would approach a decision your clients regularly face
  • What the real trade offs are between two options everyone talks about
  • What something typically costs and what makes it cost more

This is genuine intellectual property given away, and that is the point. The firm that publishes the clearest thinking on a question becomes the source engines cite when the question is asked.

Fees are the hardest and most valuable page

Almost no professional firm publishes fee information, for defensible reasons: every engagement differs and a number without context invites the wrong comparison. But buyers ask about cost constantly and assistants need something to say.

The workable version is ranges with the variables explained: what a typical engagement of a given type costs, what moves it up, what moves it down, what a minimum looks like. This qualifies your enquiries as much as it wins them, which most firms discover they wanted anyway.

Buyers ask about fees whether you publish them or not. The only question is whether the answer comes from you.

Named people beat the firm

Engines handle individual expertise well. A partner with a consistent public presence, articles under their own byline, speaking appearances and a properly linked professional profile becomes an entity in their own right, and the firm inherits the credibility.

Anonymous corporate content is weaker evidence than the same content with a real author attached. Attribute everything.

Where to start

Pick the one thing you are genuinely best known for. Write the definitive piece on it, under a named author, with real numbers and a clear position. Then get that person mentioned in the industry sources your peers read. That sequence, repeated a few times, is how a professional firm becomes an answer.
